After more than three decades in hiatus, KUSU KUSU—one of the most innovative acts to emerge from Japan's explosive 1980s band boom—have officially returned. Vocalist Kawagami Jiro recently spoke about the band's resurrection, revealing how a landmark 35th anniversary milestone sparked a creative rebirth that has already captured the hearts of thousands.

KUSU KUSU were never quite like anything else. While their contemporaries rode the wave of idol-adjacent band fever that swept Japan in the late '80s—fuelled by TV talent show sensations, pedestrian shopping street phenomena, and an underground indie explosion—KUSU KUSU carved their own path by blending world music, rock, and pop into something genuinely distinctive. Despite critical acclaim and rapid ascent to the prestigious Nippon Budokan, the band grew frustrated with being treated as idols rather than serious musicians. Four years after their major label debut, they indefinitely suspended all activity.

**The Return**

When 2025 rolled around marking 35 years since their debut, the four members—now scattered across Japan with bassist Makoto living in Okinawa—began regular video meetings to discuss possibilities. Rather than reunite physically (logistically challenging given their current lives), they devised a hybrid approach: Jiro would headline as KUSU KUSU's public face, while the other three contributed composition, songwriting, and studio arrangements behind the scenes.

A crowdfunding campaign launched to finance new material achieved an astonishing 1,324% of its target. The response shocked even the band themselves. More significantly, it proved something unexpected: the music industry and fans alike had finally begun judging KUSU KUSU on artistic merit rather than idol cachet. The resulting single, "Jungle Rock," released July 29th, pairs fresh material with a self-cover of their debut single "The Day the World Was Happiest"—and it's unmistakably, gloriously KUSU KUSU, brimming with the same eclectic energy that defined them decades ago.

"People are finally evaluating what we were trying to do creatively," Jiro reflected. "That's the greatest gift this comeback could offer."

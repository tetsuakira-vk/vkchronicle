Taboo stands as the definitive statement of Buck-Tick's artistic ambition during the pivotal late 1980s, a moment when visual kei was crystallizing from underground experimentation into a genuine cultural movement. Released in 1988, this album represents the band at a creative crossroads—no longer the punk revivalists of their earliest work, but not yet the electronic provocateurs they would become. Instead, Taboo captures something rarer: a fully formed artistic vision marrying gothic atmospherics, psychedelic rock traditions, and the theatrical darkness that would come to define visual kei at its most compelling.

The sonic palette Atsushi Sakurai and his bandmates construct here is immediately distinctive. Hisashi Imai's guitar work oscillates between crystalline, almost glassy textures and heavy, distorted passages that recall both post-punk and heavy metal without fully committing to either. This duality—lightness and darkness coexisting—becomes the album's defining characteristic. The rhythm section of Yutaka Higuchi and Toll Yagami provides a foundation that's simultaneously tight and slightly unmoored, lending tracks an eerie instability. The production, warm yet somehow unsettling, bathes everything in an amber haze that feels deliberately retro and genuinely contemporary simultaneously.

Sakurai's vocal performance deserves particular attention. His range throughout Taboo is extraordinary, moving from whispered, almost conversational passages to soaring, androgynous wails that cut through the mix with genuine emotional intensity. He never sounds like he's performing for effect alone; even when operating within visual kei's theatrical conventions, his delivery carries real conviction. The lyrical content, while characteristically enigmatic, orbits themes of desire, taboo transgression, and psychological discord—exactly the subject matter a band of this sophistication should tackle.

Standout tracks reveal the album's conceptual richness. The opening salvo establishes an immediately gothic atmosphere, while subsequent numbers like the hypnotic "Raven" showcase the band's ability to build tension across extended arrangements. Mid-album, Imai's guitar work becomes increasingly inventive, layering textures that suggest everything from Cocteau Twins to early Siouxsie and the Banshees. The band never plagiarizes these influences; rather, they've clearly internalized Western gothic rock and filtered it through a distinctly Japanese sensibility—more theatrical, more technologically interested, more willing to embrace artifice as genuine artistic expression.

Within Buck-Tick's own discography, Taboo represents a pivotal moment. Their earlier work showed promise but occasional naïveté; their later output would see them venture into industrial territory with even greater confidence. Taboo is the perfect balance—ambitious but grounded, experimental yet accessible enough for listeners to actually engage with the songs rather than merely the concept. In the broader visual kei context, this album demonstrated that the movement could produce work of genuine artistic depth, challenging the notion that VK was purely aesthetic posturing.

That challenge remains relevant today. Taboo rewards close listening and repeated exposure in ways that many 1980s rock albums simply don't. The production has aged beautifully, the songs have weathered decades without sounding dated, and Sakurai's performance feels transcendent rather than merely theatrical.

New listeners should approach Taboo as a masterclass in visual kei's most compelling possibilities: the marriage of genuine musicianship, conceptual ambition, and theatrical presentation. This is essential listening for anyone seeking to understand not just Buck-Tick, but the entire visual kei movement's artistic foundations.

**Rating: 9/10**
